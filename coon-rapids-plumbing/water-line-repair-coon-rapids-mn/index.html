    Underground Leak Detection in Coon Rapids, MN

    A ground microphone picks up the hiss of water escaping a pressurized pipe, even through a couple of feet of dirt. Some leaks barely make a sound. For those we push a hydrogen and nitrogen tracer gas into the drained line and sniff for it at the surface, which finds leaks the microphone cannot hear. Glacial clay and sandy soils are both common in Minnesota, and deep frost causes the ground to heave and settle each year. You get a written price as soon as the leak is marked on the ground.

    Commercial Water Line Repair in Coon Rapids, MN

    Commercial service lines are often 1 1/2 to 4 inch pipe feeding a meter bank, a backflow assembly and sometimes a fire line. No water means no open sign. CNV Plumbing can schedule repairs early in the morning or after closing, and we bring the larger fittings and couplings on the truck.     Repair vs. Replace Your Water Line in Coon Rapids, MN

    One leak on a newer copper or PEX line is a spot repair. Repeated leaks on galvanized pipe call for a new line. Age matters too, since most service lines past 40 years old are living on borrowed time. Spot repairs often run a few hundred dollars while a full replacement costs more and resets the clock for decades. We show you the pipe we exposed.

    Pressure Regulator and Shutoff Repair in Coon Rapids

    High static pressure shortens the life of the service line and every fixture behind it. Most codes cap it at 80 psi. We gauge the pressure at a hose bib, then swap a failed regulator for a new Watts or Zurn Wilkins valve when it reads too high. Old gate valves that will not close get replaced with full-port ball valves. That way you can actually shut the water off.

    PEX Water Line Repair in Coon Rapids, MN

    PEX itself rarely fails underground, but the joints and the first few feet near the house take a beating. Rodents chew it.     Pipe Bursting and Pulling in Coon Rapids

    Pipe bursting means splitting the old line in place while the new one follows behind it. Straight runs are ideal. A cone-shaped head breaks the old pipe outward, while new HDPE comes through right behind the head. You end up with a fresh line and two small pits, and the lawn, sidewalk and driveway in between stay where they are, which is the whole reason people pick this method over a trench.
