    Under-Sink Grease Trap Maintenance in Blaine, MN

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps are rated by flow, usually 10 to 50 gallons per minute, and they hold far less than people expect. Busy kitchens need them cleaned every week or two.     Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Blaine

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. Large outdoor interceptors can pass $1,000 when they are full and the cap has hardened. The biggest variable is the gallons hauled, because disposal facilities charge by volume and a 1,500-gallon tank is a full truckload.

    Under-sink traps take 30 to 60 minutes. Plan one to three hours for an outdoor interceptor. Big tanks with a hardened cap take the longest, since the cap has to be broken up with a pressure wand before the hose will pull it.

    Fryer volume drives the schedule more than seat count. Plenty of busy restaurants pump monthly. Lighter kitchens, like a coffee shop with a small food menu, can stretch to every two or three months as long as depth readings stay under the 25 percent line.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Most people use trap for the small indoor unit, usually under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.

    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. You do need to keep the records. A new or resized trap is different, usually needing a plumbing permit and sometimes a review by the pretreatment program first.

    Slow sinks come first. Gurgling floor drains and a sour smell by the dish station usually follow. By the time gray water is coming up through a floor sink, the trap is usually well past 25 percent full and grease is already heading down the line.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. Haulers call it brown grease. It is different from the yellow grease in your used fryer oil bin, which is recycled separately and should never be poured into the trap.

    It is the most common pumping standard. When grease and solids reach a quarter of the depth, it is time to pump. Beyond that level, water passes through too quickly to separate, and grease leaves through the outlet even though the trap seems fine.
