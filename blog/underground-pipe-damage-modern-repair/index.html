    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

    What Goes Wrong Under the Yard

    Your sewer lateral and water service run from the house to the street, usually somewhere 2 to 6 feet underground. Nobody thinks about them until a toilet backs up or the lawn turns soggy. Most laterals are 4-inch, and on older houses that usually means clay or cast iron. Each fails in its own way.

    Common Causes

    Tree Roots

    Roots find sewer lines by following moisture from a cracked joint or a loose gasket. Once a hair-thin root gets in, it grows into a mat that catches paper and grease. Clay pipe is the worst for this. Its joints come every couple of feet, and a big maple or oak can pack a 4-inch line full in a few growing seasons.

    Shifting Soil

    Glacial clay and sandy soils are both common in Minnesota, and deep frost causes the ground to heave and settle each year.

    Soil that settles or swells moves the pipe with it. A rigid clay or cast iron line cannot flex, so it cracks or pulls apart at a joint. Sags are common too. A sag, which we call a belly, holds water and waste, and on camera it looks like a stretch that stays full after flushing.

    Old Pipe Material

    Mid-century houses usually have copper supply lines, while newer construction relies on PEX and plastic drains.

    Age wears every material down. Cast iron rusts from the inside, and the bottom of the pipe can corrode through while the top still looks solid. Orangeburg, which builders used into the early 1970s, is tar-soaked fiber pipe that slowly squashes flat. Old galvanized water lines rust shut inside and drop the pressure everywhere.

    Signs of a Buried Line Problem

    Several drains slowing at once points to the main line. One classic sign is a toilet that gurgles as the washer empties. Outside, look for a patch of grass that stays greener than the rest, or soft ground over the path of the line, especially in dry weather. Sewer smell near the cleanout is another.

    On the water side, the meter tells the story. Turn off every fixture and watch the leak indicator, the small triangle or dial on the meter face. A spinning indicator means water is leaving somewhere between the meter and the house, and a buried service line is a likely spot. Low pressure throughout the house can mean the same.

    A sewer camera settles it. We run a camera through the cleanout and record the whole line, with a locator that marks depth and position on the ground above. We show you the footage as it plays. The footage decides the repair.

    Modern Repair Options

    Trench repair is still the right call for some jobs. A collapsed section or a line with a deep belly usually has to be dug up, since lining needs a pipe that still holds its shape. Spot repairs can be small. A single broken joint under the yard might mean a 4 by 6 foot hole and a day of work.

    Trenchless lining avoids most of the digging. We pull a resin-soaked felt liner into the old pipe, inflate it, and let it cure into a new pipe inside the old one. Usually it goes in through the cleanout plus one small pit, and it is done in a day. Roots cannot get through it.

    Pipe bursting is the other trenchless method. A cone-shaped head is winched through the old pipe, splitting it and pulling new HDPE pipe into place behind it. It needs a pit at each end but leaves the yard between them alone. Our crews at CNV Plumbing use both methods across Minnesota, depending on what the camera shows.

    When to Call

    Do not wait on a sewer line that has backed up twice. Every backup leaves more waste behind, and that feeds the roots. Most of our camera inspections take under an hour, and you get a copy of the video plus a marked-up location of the problem. Reach CNV Plumbing at (612) 712-9569. Ask about the cleanout location when you call, since an accessible cleanout saves time.
