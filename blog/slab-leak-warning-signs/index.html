    Slab Leak Warning Signs Every Minnesota Homeowner Should Know

    By Aaron Mitchell · October 15, 2026 · 5 min read

    What a Slab Leak Actually Is

    A lot of houses built since the 1960s sit on a poured concrete slab, with copper or PEX supply lines and cast iron or PVC drains running through or under the concrete. There is nothing wrong with building a house that way. A slab leak starts when one of those buried lines wears a pinhole, splits at a fitting or cracks along its length, and water starts moving into the concrete or the soil below it. Some leak a cup a day, others leak gallons an hour.

    Freeze and thaw movement can shift older sewer laterals, and tree roots often find their way into the joints.

    Soil under any slab swells when it gets wet and shrinks when it dries, and that cycle shifts the concrete by fractions of an inch over the years. Buried pipe handles that movement poorly. Copper rubs against rebar or gravel, joints get pulled, and a line that was fine for twenty years gives out one ordinary afternoon.

    Warning Signs Worth Taking Seriously

    A Water Bill That Jumps for No Reason

    Pull up the last six months of bills and compare gallons, not dollars. A jump in gallons with no change in habits means water is going somewhere. A pinhole in a 3/4-inch copper line under normal house pressure can push out several hundred gallons a day, and the meter will show it long before you see a drop anywhere in the house. Shut off every fixture and watch the little leak dial on the meter.

    Warm Spots on the Floor

    Walk the house barefoot. A hot water line leaking under the slab warms the concrete above it, and you will feel a patch of tile or laminate that stays warm while the rest of the floor is cool. Dogs and cats often find the warm spot before you do. A thermal imaging camera shows the leak path in a few minutes.

    Running Water With Every Tap Closed

    Late at night, the house finally goes quiet. Kneel in a hallway or closet and listen close to the floor. A hiss or faint rushing sound with every faucet, toilet and appliance shut off usually means pressurized water is escaping somewhere below the slab. A toilet that cycles on its own is a different issue, usually a worn flapper.

    Pressure That Drops Off

    Weak flow at one faucet is usually a clogged aerator. Low pressure at every fixture points somewhere else. When a supply line under the slab is broken, part of the water headed for your shower is going into the dirt instead, so pressure sags across the whole house and gets worse when two fixtures run at once. We test static pressure at a hose bib with a gauge.

    New Cracks in Walls or Floors

    Water moving under a slab washes out fine soil and softens clay, and over a few months the concrete above it can settle unevenly. You start seeing cracked tile grout, drywall cracks over door frames and baseboards pulling away from the wall. Doors that used to close fine start sticking. At that point the leak is also a foundation problem.

    Mold, Mildew and Musty Carpet

    A musty smell in one room, or carpet pad that stays damp along an interior wall, is a common sign that a slab leak has been wicking moisture up through the concrete for a while. Mildew tends to appear low on the drywall and behind the trim. Cleaning it without finding the water only buys a few weeks.

    Soggy Spots Outside the Foundation

    Some leaks push water sideways until it surfaces. Watch for a patch of lawn next to the house that stays green and soft when everything else is dry. Puddles that never dry, soil pulling away from the footing, or a flower bed that is always mud may point to a broken line under the slab.

    When to Call a Professional

    One warning sign by itself can be a coincidence. Two or three at once rarely are. Before you reach CNV Plumbing at (612) 712-9569, shut off every fixture, read the water meter, wait an hour without running anything and read it again. If the numbers moved, the leak is on the pressurized side.

    Why Detection Gear Matters

    Finding a slab leak is a locating job before it is a repair job. We use acoustic listening gear, a thermal camera, an isolation pressure test and a line tracer to mark the leak within a foot or so. That precision matters because the repair options, a spot repair through the slab or a reroute through the walls and ceilings, both depend on knowing exactly where the break sits and what kind of pipe it is. Guessing means more broken concrete.

    Rerouting is often the better call on older copper. One pinhole in a 40-year-old line is rarely the last one. A new PEX run through the walls and ceilings skips the concrete altogether, and most homes are back on water the same day or the next, with a few drywall patches as the only cleanup left behind.
